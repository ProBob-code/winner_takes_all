# The league-night playbook

**How to start a weekly competition in a room that has never had one.**

Written for: a venue owner or manager with a pool table, a bar, and no league.

---

## Why this document exists before any feature

Twenty venue owners were interviewed about Winner Takes All. Ten said they
would buy it. Of the ten who would not, three gave the same reason, and one of
them put it best:

> *"I would be buying a scoreboard for an event that does not exist yet."*

They did not object to the price, the product or the idea. They had no
competition night to score. No feature answers that. A guide to starting one
might, which is why this was written before the offline scoring, the big-screen
display or the QR entry — all of which are useless to a room with nothing on
the calendar.

Nothing in here needs Winner Takes All. It is a guide to running a league
night. We would rather you ran one on paper than not at all.

---

## What you are actually building

Not a tournament. A **habit**.

A one-off tournament brings people in once and tells you nothing. A weekly
night, on the same day, at the same time, for six weeks, does something
different: by week four people arrange their Thursday around it. That is the
asset. It survives a bad week, a broken table and a staff change, and it keeps
earning long after the novelty is gone.

The whole plan below is built around protecting the habit, not maximising any
single night.

**The number that matters:** eight regulars who come most weeks beats forty
people once. Eight is enough for a bracket, enough for atmosphere, and small
enough that they all know each other by week three — which is what makes them
come back.

---

## Before week one: the four decisions

Make these once. Changing them later costs you the habit.

### 1. The day, and never move it

Pick your **second-quietest** night.

Not the quietest — that is usually a night nobody wants to be out at all. Not a
busy one either, because you will resent giving the table up. The
second-quietest is a night with some life in it and room to grow.

Once chosen, do not move it. "League night is Thursday" is a sentence your
regulars can repeat to other people. "League night is usually Thursday, but
this week…" is not.

### 2. The time, and make it late enough

Start at 8pm or later. People eat first. A 7pm start means a trickle until
7.40 and a bracket that does not fill, which looks like a failed night when it
is only an early one.

### 3. The format, and keep it short

**Single elimination, best of one frame, eight players.** Nothing else, to
start.

An eight-player knockout is seven frames. At ten minutes a frame on one table
that is seventy minutes plus the gaps — about two hours all in. That fits one
evening, finishes before people want to leave, and produces a winner.

Resist round-robin for the first month. It is a better format and it takes
three times as long, and a night that overruns is a night people make excuses
about next week.

### 4. The prize, and keep it out of money

This matters more than it looks.

**Do not pool entry fees.** India's Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming
Act 2025, in force since 1 May 2026, prohibits games played for a stake whether
they turn on skill or on chance. Collecting ₹100 a head and paying the winner
₹700 is the exact structure the Act prohibits, however you describe it.

So make entry free, and make the prize something you already sell:

- A bar tab — ₹500 behind the bar, for the winner
- A free round for the winner's table
- A month of free table hire
- A trophy that lives on your shelf with the winner's name on it

The trophy is underrated. It costs you ₹800 once and it is the thing people
photograph. A bar tab returns to your till. Both are better business than
handing out cash, and neither is a stake.

> **What a venue does at its own counter is its own affair.** This document is
> about not building a pooled-stake prize into the structure of the night. If
> you are unsure how the law applies to you, ask someone who can advise you
> properly — nothing here is legal advice.

## Week one: eight people

Your only job this week is **eight players in the bracket**. Not a good
tournament. Eight players.

### Where they come from

Ask them. In person, at the table, in the week beforehand.

The single highest-converting thing you can do is walk up to someone already
playing pool in your venue and say: *"We're starting a knockout on Thursday at
eight. Free to enter, winner gets a ₹500 tab. Are you in?"*

That is it. Do this twenty times in the week before and you will have your
eight. It works because you are asking someone who is, at that exact moment,
demonstrably interested in playing pool in your venue.

### What does not work in week one

- **Posters alone.** A poster tells people something is happening. It does not
  make them commit. Use posters to support the asking, not to replace it.
- **Social media alone.** Your followers are mostly not in the room, and the
  ones who are already heard you ask.
- **Waiting for a good week.** There is no good week. Week one is always
  awkward and a bit thin. Run it anyway.

### The one thing to prepare

A list of names and a way to draw them. A sheet of paper works. Write the eight
names, pair them off, play.

### Expect this to go imperfectly

Six will turn up instead of eight. Run a six-player bracket. Two byes, nobody
minds. The night happening at all is worth more than the night being tidy.

---

## Week two: the same eight, plus two

The temptation after week one is to change something. Don't.

Week two's job is to prove the night is **real** — that it happens again, on
the same day, whether or not last week was busy. That proof is what turns an
event into a habit.

### Do this week

- **Message the eight from last week, individually.** Not a group blast. "You
  playing Thursday?" to each person. This is ten minutes of work and it is the
  highest-return ten minutes in this entire document.
- **Ask the winner to defend.** People turn up to defend something. Make it
  explicit: *"You're the one to beat on Thursday."*
- **Put last week's result somewhere visible.** A whiteboard by the table with
  the winner's name. This is the first brick of the league table.

### Do not do this week

- Change the day
- Change the format
- Add a second table
- Increase the prize

## Weeks three and four: it starts to run itself

Somewhere here the night stops needing you to recruit for it. The signals:

- Someone asks you what time it starts, without you mentioning it
- A player brings a friend
- Two people who met at the league are playing on a different night

### What to add now, and only now

**A running league table.** Points across weeks, not just a winner each night.
This is the single change that converts attenders into regulars, because it
gives someone who lost in the first round a reason to come back next week: they
are still in the table.

A simple scheme works: 3 points for entering, 1 per frame won, 5 for winning
the night. Do not over-engineer it. The purpose is a reason to return, not a
fair ranking.

**A season with an end.** Run the table for eight weeks, then crown a champion
and start again. An open-ended table is a table nobody can win. A season has a
run-in, and a run-in has drama.

### If it has not caught by week four

Be honest about which of these it is:

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than four turning up | Not enough asking | Go back to week one. Ask twenty people in person. |
| People come once, not twice | The night overran, or nothing to come back for | Shorten the format. Start the league table. |
| Same four every week, no growth | Too closed, or clique-forming | Ask the four to each bring one person. Make the draw public. |
| Nobody at all | Wrong night | Move it once — and only once — then commit again. |

A night that has not caught by week six on its second day probably is not going
to. That is a fact worth knowing early; it costs six evenings to learn.

## Weeks five to eight: making it worth money

This is the point where the night starts producing the thing you actually
wanted — spend, on a night that did not have any.

### What to measure

Three numbers, written down each week:

1. **Players in the bracket.** The health of the night.
2. **Heads in the room at 9pm.** Players bring spectators; spectators drink.
   This is usually two to three times the bracket by week six.
3. **Till take, against the same weekday a month earlier.** The only number
   that answers whether this was worth doing.

The third is the one to look at honestly. If a league night of sixteen players
and forty heads has not moved your Thursday take, something else is wrong —
usually that the night is running too late to drink or too early to stay.

### The two upgrades worth making

**A screen.** Once there are spectators, a bracket on a wall changes the room:
people watch, people know when they are up, nobody has to ask. This is where
Winner Takes All's display mode earns its keep — but a printed bracket on the
wall, updated with a marker, does most of the work.

**Someone other than you scoring.** The night cannot depend on you being there.
Train one member of staff to run it. If they cannot pick it up in ninety
seconds, the way you are running it is too complicated.

> Six of the twenty owners we interviewed named exactly this — staff being able
> to run it without them — as the thing standing between them and buying
> anything. It is a real constraint and it is worth designing the night around.

## The one-page version

Print this bit.

```
BEFORE WEEK 1
  Pick your second-quietest night. Never move it.
  Start at 8pm or later.
  Single elimination, best of one, eight players.
  Prize = a bar tab or a trophy. Never pooled cash.

WEEK 1
  Ask twenty people, in person, at the table.
  Run it with six if six turn up.

WEEK 2
  Message last week's eight individually.
  Ask the winner to defend.
  Put the result on a whiteboard.
  Change nothing else.

WEEKS 3-4
  Start a league table. 3 to enter, 1 per frame, 5 for the night.
  Give the season an end — eight weeks, then a champion.

WEEKS 5-8
  Count: bracket size, heads at 9pm, till take vs last month.
  Get a screen up.
  Train one member of staff to run it without you.

IF IT HASN'T CAUGHT BY WEEK 6
  Fewer than four? Not enough asking.
  Come once, not twice? Too long, or nothing to return for.
  Nobody at all? Wrong night. Move once, then commit.
```
