# The board's verdict on Winner Takes All

**Vote: 2 fund-if, 1 pass. Average score 3.3 / 10.**

Three members read the same brief through three published frameworks. They
disagreed about whether this is salvageable. They did not disagree about why it
currently is not.

## The risks more than one member raised

These go first because independent lenses reached them independently.

**1. The thing being sold is the prohibited thing.** All three said it in
different words. The Offers lens: "the stake *is* the offer" — remove it and the
dream outcome is deleted, not weakened. The Product lens: the stake is "the
spine, with the name welded to it." The Monopoly lens: it is "the exact structure
the Act bans, skill defence explicitly removed." This is not a feature behind a
flag. The product is named after it.

**2. The skill disclaimer is worse than useless.** Two members flagged the
"outcomes are determined solely by participant skill" notice unprompted. The
2025 Act removed that defence explicitly. The Offers lens called it "not
protection, a record of intent." The Product lens called it "a legal fiction
stitched over the UI." It evidences that the founder knew which defence he was
relying on.

**3. The payment rail belongs to somebody else.** Banks and PSPs are barred from
processing for money games. Razorpay is, as the Monopoly lens put it, "a single
switch somebody else owns." No amount of product work changes this.

**4. Distribution is absent at both ends, and one end is closed by statute.**
Advertising money games is banned, so the current model has no legal paid
channel. Five accounts, word of mouth. The lawful version proposes no channel
at all yet.

**5. The economics cannot carry the risk.** ₹28 gross on a ₹400 pot, against
personal exposure of three years and ₹1 crore. Two members made the same
observation: friends settling a bet in cash already pay 0%, instantly.

## Where they disagreed

The Monopoly lens voted **PASS** and meant it: in the lawful version there is no
monopoly and no revenue line, the gate is licensing rather than engineering, and
licensing is won by institutional relationships nobody has named.

The other two voted **FUND IF** — but for a *different business*: the buyer
changes from player to host, and the asset is reused rather than extended. The
Offers lens was explicit that this "is not a pivot of an offer; it is an
admission that the offer must be replaced."

All three rated the engineering highly and the business poorly. That gap is the
finding.

## The merged conditions

A checklist. The CFO, pricing and consumer panel test most of these.

- [ ] **Stop taking stakes now.** Return wallet balances. Not conditional on
      anything else being decided.
- [ ] **Delete the skill-based disclaimer.** It asserts a defence that no longer
      exists and documents intent.
- [ ] **Take written Indian counsel**, including on tournaments already run and
      on whether personal or corporate liability attaches.
- [ ] **Remove participant money from the platform entirely** — no wallet, no
      rake, no payouts. Any prize comes from a venue or sponsor and is never
      pooled from entrants.
- [ ] **Pick one customer.** The build currently serves both player and
      organiser. Two members independently said: the organiser.
- [ ] **Ship a no-stake version and prove people still play.** The measure is
      completed tournaments per week with zero rupees staked. If the experience
      only works with money on it, the product was the money.
- [ ] **Retire the name along with the model.** "Winner Takes All" advertises
      the banned structure.
- [ ] **Find three to ten paying hosts by hand before another feature is
      written.** If three cannot be found, the offer is wrong, not the price.
- [ ] **No customer-facing moment may wait on the founder being awake.**

## The strongest version of this business the board can see

Not a tournament platform that takes a cut of the pot. **Software a venue pays
for, to run its own competition night.**

The organiser — a gaming café, a pool hall, a community league — pays a flat
monthly fee. They get brackets, live 8-ball and football scoring on the café
screen, weekly series with standings that bring regulars back, QR broadcast and
streaming, and an arena mode for local score-keeping. No participant money ever
touches the platform: entry is free or collected by the venue in its own name,
and prizes come from the venue or a sponsor.

This is lawful on its face, it needs no Online Gaming Authority licence while no
stake is involved, it bills a business rather than a consumer, and it keeps the
part of the asset that took the most skill to build — the scoring engine, the
series standings, the streaming, the arena — while deleting the wallet, the
rake, the settlement and the payout queue.

It is also, plainly, a different company with a different buyer and no proof of
demand yet. The board is saying the asset is worth more than the business, and
that the founder is worth more than both.
